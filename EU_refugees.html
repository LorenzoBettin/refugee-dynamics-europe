Introduction¶

In recent years, Europe has been facing a dual trend with significant humanitarian and political implications: an increasing influx of refugees (Schilling et al., 2017) and a notable shift in the political landscape towards right-wing ideologies. The rising number of refugees arriving in Europe can largely be attributed to escalating conflicts, political instability, and humanitarian crises in regions like the Middle East (Doraï, 2018), Africa, and parts of Asia. Wars, persecution, and economic collapse have forced millions of individuals and families to flee their homes in search of safety and stability (Lewtak et al., 2022), with Europe often seen as a refuge offering protection and a fresh start. Climate change, too, has compounded these pressures, leading to displacement as people in affected areas face resource scarcity, crop failure, and extreme weather events (Myers, 2002). The result is an urgent influx of refugees seeking shelter within European borders, where countries must determine how to support this vulnerable population.

At the same time, European politics has seen a marked shift towards right-wing ideologies, a trend influenced by economic anxieties, cultural identity debates, and concerns over national security. Many political parties and leaders in Europe, responding to public fears and frustrations, have adopted rhetoric and policies that focus on protecting national interests and restricting immigration (Dennison, 2019). In this context, refugees are often depicted as a burden on the economy or a threat to national identity, with right-leaning parties using them as scapegoats to address broader concerns about unemployment, housing shortages, and public safety (Anthony, 2024). This political shift can directly impact national policies, which may, in turn, determine the number of refugees each country is willing to accept and support.

This political narrative not only impacts public opinion but may also correlate with the number of refugees each country accepts or supports. To better anticipate how countries might approach housing refugees, it is essential to understand how political orientation and specific policies might affect refugee influxes. This leads us to our research questions.

Research Question and Sub-questions¶

The main research question is as follows:

What is the effect of different factors on the amount of refugees in European countries?

To investigate this, we explore the following sub-questions:

  • What is the ratio of refugees per population size in each country?
  • What is the effect of government orientation on the amount of refugees?
  • Which core socio-economic indicators have a significant effect on the amount of refugees?

With these questions in mind, our project will delve into data-driven analysis to uncover how political orientations and socio-economic conditions influence the refugee population across Europe. This understanding could provide valuable insights for policymakers and stakeholders who are responsible for shaping sustainable and humane approaches to the current refugee situation.

Datasets¶

To explore these questions and the several effects, there are two datasets needed. The two datasets are a dataset from CDPS and a dataset from Word Bank Group. The CDPS dataset containts comparative political data of different countries, and the World bank group contains refugee population by country or territory or asylum.

1. CPDS dataset¶

Source of the Dataset¶

The CPDS (Comparative Political Data Set) dataset is provided by researchers at the University of Zurich and contains political, economic, and social data covering OECD and EU member states from 1960 to 2022. The dataset is widely used in comparative political studies and includes variables on government composition, party orientation, voter turnout, economic indicators, and social policies. Its purpose is to support the empirical analysis of political and social factors across countries over time.

Structure of the Dataset¶

The CPDS dataset is in Excel format and contains numerous rows representing yearly observations for each country, with over 150 columns covering various political, economic, and social variables. This dataset contains a comprehensive time series of various socio-economic and political indicators, spanning the years 1960 to 2022 across 36 countries. The data includes 1,866 observations with 335 variables, covering attributes such as country identifiers (name, ISO codes), year, and a variety of economic, demographic, and political metrics. The dataset is downloadable as a Stata or Excel file. The file we used in this research is an Excel file.

Variables and Data Types¶

The dataset includes variables like year (year of observation), country (country name), gov_right1, gov_cent1, and gov_left1 (party orientation variables indicating the proportion of right, center, and left-wing parties in government), kaopen (capital account openness), debt (government debt), vturn (voter turnout), pop (population), and various economic indicators such as inflation and realgdpgr (real GDP growth). These variables are a blend of numerical and categorical data, providing a robust view of each country's political, social, and economic landscape.

Due to the dataset's extensive scope, with 335 variables covering socio-economic, political, and demographic metrics, listing each individually would be impractical and would hinder readability. Instead, focusing on the main categories—like government composition, economic conditions, social policies, and demographic information—provides a concise yet comprehensive overview of the dataset’s structure and core areas of interest. This summarization helps to highlight the dataset’s capacity for enabling detailed, multidimensional analysis of political and economic trends over time. The dataset includes essential identifiers like year (integer), country (categorical), iso (categorical), and iso3n (integer), which define each entry by country and year. Demographic indicators, such as pop (float, total population), pop15_64 (float, working-age population), and pop65 (float, elderly population), provide insight into population distributions by age. Political metrics, like gov_right1 (float, proportion of right-leaning government), capture political affiliations. These key variables span primarily numerical types (integer and float) for continuous data, with categorical fields for country identifiers and political affiliations, providing a well-rounded framework for analyzing long-term socio-economic and political trends. A complete overview of this dataset can be found at https://cpds-data.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/codebook_cpds-.pdf

2. World bank dataset¶

Source of the Dataset¶

The refugee population dataset is provided by the World Bank Group, a global organization that provides financial and technical assistance to developing countries. The World Bank gathers and maintains various social, economic, and demographic datasets, including refugee data, to support development goals. This specific dataset is part of the World Bank's extensive social and demographic data collection, aimed at monitoring global refugee populations by country or territory of asylum. It is accessible on the World Bank’s online repository.

Structure of the Dataset¶

The World Bank refugee dataset, available in CSV format, provides data on refugee populations by country or territory of asylum from 1960 to 2023. With 264 rows (each representing a unique country) and 69 columns, it includes country-specific identifiers, metadata, and annual refugee population counts, allowing both cross-sectional and longitudinal analysis. Key variables include Country Name (asylum country), Country Code (unique identifier), Indicator Name ("Refugee population by country or territory of asylum"), and yearly columns for refugee_population. This numeric variable captures the refugee count per country-year, supporting trend and comparative analyses of migration patterns. A codebook from the World Bank provides definitions and methodologies for each variable, ensuring accurate data interpretation, which is critical for studies in migration and development policy.

Variables and Data Types¶

The variables of the dataset from World Bank are the following: Country Name = Name of the country or territory of asylum for refugees. the country name tye is string (categorical) Country Code = ISO 3-letter code for each country or territory. The country code type is string (categorical) Indicator Name = Description of the indicator (e.g., "Refugee population by country or territory of asylum"). String (categorical) is the data type for the Indicator name variable Indicator Code = Code representing the specific indicator (e.g., SM.POP.REFG). String (categorical) is the type of data for this variable. 1960-2023 = Refugee population count for each year from 1960 to 2023. Each column represents a different year. As for the years variable, the type is float (numerical) Unnamed: 68 An empty or extra column likely due to formatting; it contains null values. Therefore are not to be considered.

This dataset paints a picture of where refugees have found asylum over time. Each Country Name and Country Code represents a place of refuge, while the Indicator Name and Indicator Code specify that this data is about refugee populations in each country or territory. The core of the dataset spans 1960 to 2023, with each year column showing the refugee population in that specific year. This data reveals how global events and policies have influenced where refugees have settled over the decades It is possible to access the codebook and additional information on variable definitions on the World Bank Data Catalog at https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SM.POP.REFG. Step 5 Imputing Missing Values: Finally, any remaining missing values in the selected features are imputed using the mean strategy. This ensures that no missing data interferes with the analysis, resulting in a fully prepared and clean dataset for modeling.

Machine learning¶

This code follows a comprehensive approach to preparing, training, evaluating, and cross-validating a linear regression model to predict refugee populations. It starts by preparing the dataset, filtering columns to include only relevant numerical and categorical variables. Categorical variables are converted to a numeric format through dummy encoding, which is essential for integrating them effectively into the regression model. Missing values in the dataset are imputed using the mean strategy, ensuring that all features are complete and usable for modeling, which minimizes data loss and enhances model reliability.

Once data preprocessing is complete, the dataset is split into training and test sets to allow for independent evaluation of the model’s predictive accuracy. A constant term is added to the model to capture baseline effects, and an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression is fitted using the training data. After training, the model’s performance is evaluated on the test set using Mean Squared Error (MSE) and R-squared metrics, which provide insights into prediction accuracy and explained variance. To further assess model generalizability, cross-validation is performed using R-squared scores across multiple folds, with an average score calculated to summarize the model’s predictive stability across different data subsets. This structured approach ensures the model is both robustly trained and rigorously validated, balancing accuracy with the prevention of overfitting.

Discussion¶

Interpret your results in relation to your research question. Were there any surprises in your research? How reliable do you think your results are? Are there any limitations to your analysis?

This study examines how economic, social, and political factors affect refugee populations in European and other big western countries. Using an OLS regression model, the analysis yields an R-squared of 0.600, explaining a moderate level of variance in refugee numbers across countries. Below, we address each sub-research question in turn.

  1. What is the ratio of refugees per population size in each country? The dataset findings show varying ratios of refugees to population size across Europe, with countries experiencing higher refugee-to-population ratios often being those with smaller populations but close proximity to regions of conflict or transit routes. For example, countries like Cyprus and Estonia display a high refugee-to-population ratio despite modest absolute refugee numbers. This suggests that smaller European nations may face proportionally greater impacts from refugee flows relative to their population sizes.

  2. What is the effect of government orientation on the number of refugees? Government orientation, measured here by gov_type and other political variables, shows limited significance in predicting refugee numbers. While political orientation might be expected to influence refugee policies, this model indicates that external factors, such as regional conflicts, may play a stronger role in shaping refugee inflows than government structure alone. This outcome implies that while domestic policies contribute to a country’s overall stance, they may not substantially alter the volume of refugees compared to larger geopolitical pressures.

  3. Which core socio-economic indicators have a significant effect on the number of refugees? Key socio-economic indicators, especially trade openness, labor force size, and unemployment rate, emerge as significant predictors of refugee populations. Trade openness and labor force size positively correlate with refugee numbers, suggesting that economically open countries with robust labor markets may attract more refugees due to perceived stability and opportunities. In contrast, high unemployment correlates negatively with refugee numbers, possibly signaling economic challenges that may deter refugee arrivals. Surprisingly, debt levels did not show significance, implying that overall economic capacity matters more than debt burdens in influencing refugee inflows.

Main Research Question:

What is the effect of different factors on the number of refugees in European countries? This analysis suggests that socio-economic conditions, particularly trade openness and labor market size, play a prominent role in attracting refugee populations to European countries. In contrast, political structures like government orientation have a secondary, less direct effect. The results underscore that a country’s economic health and stability often outweigh its political organization in shaping refugee inflows, though regional pressures remain decisive. Surprises, Reliability, and Limitations: A notable surprise was the minimal impact of political orientation on refugee numbers, highlighting that external factors like neighboring conflicts may have a more immediate influence. Model reliability is moderate, though cross-validation R-squared scores are negative, indicating limited predictive consistency. This outcome suggests that unobserved factors or model limitations—including multicollinearity among predictors—affect results. Additionally, the linear model may oversimplify refugee dynamics, which are likely influenced by complex, non-linear interactions of economic, social, and geopolitical factors.

In conclusion, while socio-economic factors are central to understanding refugee populations in Europe, future research could benefit from more advanced models to capture the complex interplay of domestic conditions and regional pressures affecting refugee flows.

Conclusion¶

This research sheds light on the significant role of socio-economic factors in shaping refugee populations across European countries. Key indicators, such as trade openness, labor force size, and unemployment, emerged as central predictors, highlighting that countries with robust economies and labor markets are more likely to attract refugees. Conversely, political orientation, though initially expected to have a strong influence, demonstrated limited direct impact, suggesting that while domestic policies play a role, external factors and regional stability may exert greater influence over refugee inflows. This insight underscores the multifaceted nature of migration and the importance of combining economic and geopolitical perspectives to understand refugee dynamics.
